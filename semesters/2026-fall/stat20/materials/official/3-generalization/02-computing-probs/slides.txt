The champion is a better player than Elmer’s father. Which series should Elmer choose: father-champion-father or champion-father-champion? You may treat the result of each game within a set as independent of the other two games.

Concept Review
Rules for computing probabilities
Conditional Probability:
For two events \(A\) and \(B\), \(P(A \vert B) = \displaystyle \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}\)
Multiplication rule:
For two events \(A\) and \(B\), \(P(A \cap B) = P(A \textbf{ and } B)= P(A \vert B) P(B)\)
Independent events:
We say that two non-empty events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\times P(B)\).
Complement rule:
\(P(A^C) = 1 - P(A)\)
Addition rule (events not necessarily mutually exclusive):
\(P(A\cup B) = P(A \textbf{ or } B)= P(A) + P(B) - P(A\cap B)\)
I like to tell them for conditional probability that their “universe” (outcome space) changes with the information, so now all the probabilities have to be adjusted. That is what conditioning on this new information means. We now have a new probability function on this new outcome space \(B\).
You can tell them here that \(A^C\) is “not A”, and then maybe use dice or coin toss examples. Review mutually exclusive vs independent events, emphasizing that these are very different ideas, though both apply to pairs of events.
Mutually exclusive events means that the occurrence of one event prevents the occurrence of the other. (that is, it reduces the chance of the other occurring to 0.)
Independent events means that the occurrence of one event does not change the chance of the other occurring.
For the addition rule, ask them what happens if the sets are mutually exclusive.

Toss three coins, one at a time. Define the following events:
\(A\) is the event that the first coin flipped shows a head.
\(B\) is the event that the first two coins flipped both show heads.
\(C\) is the event that the last two coins flipped both show tails.
Are the events \(A\) and \(B\) independent, mutually exclusive or neither?
What about \(A\) and \(C\)? What about \(B\) and \(C\)?
After they are done, write out the outcome space for the results of flipping 3 coins. And go through what are A and B, C, then easy to answer all three questions.
A and B are neither independent nor mutually exclusive, in fact \(B \subset A\).
A and C are independent, and B and C are mutually exclusive.
Testing positive for a rare condition: should you be freaked out?

A rare condition affects 0.2% of the population. A test for this condition is 99% accurate: this means that the probability that a person with the condition tests positive is 99% and the probability that a person without the condition tests negative is 99%. Is the probability that a person who tests positive has the condition more or less than \(50\%\)?
First, DON’T do Bayes’ theorem, but show them how to think about the chances, and where the positive tests come from. We can think about the number of cases in 100,000, then the numbers are nice and whole.
Since prevalence is 0.2%, then 0.2% of 100,000 is 0.002*100000 = 200 people will have the condition, and 99% or 198 of them are expected to test positive. Out of the remaining (100000-200 = 99800) people who do not have the condition, 1% will test positive, which is 998 people, so the total number of positive tests is 998+198 = 1198. The proportion of true positives is 198/1198 = about 16.55% cases or about between 16% and 17% of the positives are true positives (if we use Bayes’ rule formally, it works out to be 16.55%).
