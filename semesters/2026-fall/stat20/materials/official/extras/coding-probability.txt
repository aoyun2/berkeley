Function reference
Distribution |
\(P(X = x)\) |
\(P(X \le x)\) |
Random draws |
\(Bin(n, p)\) |
dbinom(x, size, prob) |
pbinom(q, size, prob) |
rbinom(n, size, prob) |
\(HG(N, G, n)\)† |
dhyper(x, m, n, k) |
phyper(q, m, n, k) |

\(Poisson(\lambda)\) |
dpois(x, lambda) |
ppois(q, lambda) |
rpois(n, lambda) |
† Watch out for the hypergeometric arguments:
m is \(G\), the number of successes in the box.
n is \(N - G\), the number of failures in the box. R’s n is not our \(n\)!
k is \(n\), the number of draws.

Question 3: At least 4 sixes
You roll a fair die 12 times. Compute the probability of rolling at least 4 sixes.

\(P(X \ge 4) = 1 - P(X \le 3)\). Using pbinom(4, ...) would be off by one, since it includes 4.

Question 5: Sixes, simulated
Simulate rolling a fair die 12 times and counting the sixes, and repeat that 10,000 times. Use your simulation to estimate the probability of rolling at least 4 sixes. Then compare your estimate to the exact answer from pbinom() (Question 3 of the first section).
[Interactive R block: webr-18-contents; attributes: {"autorun": false, "edit": true, "envir": "extra_probability_r", "eval": true}]
# Write your code here.
Expected output is below, as a hint
Your estimate should be close to, but not exactly, the true probability (below):
[1] 0.1251781
Show answer
[#cb37-1]die <- 1:6
[#cb37-2]sixes <- replicate(10000, sum(sample(die, size = 12, replace = TRUE) == 6))
[#cb37-3]mean(sixes >= 4)
sample(die, ...) == 6 gives TRUE for each six, and sum() counts the TRUEs. Then mean(sixes >= 4) is the proportion of the 10,000 experiments with at least 4 sixes.
A box of 1s and 0s also works: box <- c(1, rep(0, 5)), then sum(sample(box, size = 12, replace = TRUE)).
