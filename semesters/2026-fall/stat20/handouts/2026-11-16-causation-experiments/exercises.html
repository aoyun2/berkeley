Case Study 1: Tech Company Salary Audit (Confounding & Simpson’s Paradox)
Dataset: salaries (40 employees across Engineering & Operations)

An audit checks salary (salary_k, in \$1000s) for Group_A vs. Group_B across two departments (dept). Comparing pooled averages directly can mislead when group sizes across high-paying and lower-paying departments differ sharply.

lm(salary_k ~ group + dept) coefficient on groupGroup_B: +4.30k
Question 1.1

Group_B earns a higher mean salary inside Engineering and inside Operations, yet a lower mean salary overall. How is that possible?

Show answer
This is Simpson’s paradox driven by confounding by dept. Engineering pays ~\$149k vs. ~\$93k in Operations, and 18/22 of Group_A works in Engineering vs. only 4/18 of Group_B. The pooled average mixes department composition with within-department pay.
Question 1.2

Explain the causal role of dept when auditing pay disparities: why does conditioning on dept answer a different question than the unadjusted difference?

Show answer
dept is a confounder for within-department pay equality (affecting both salary level and the share in each group). Conditioning on dept (via stratification or lm(salary_k ~ group + dept)) isolates equal pay for equal department work (+4.30k for Group_B), whereas any gap in department access itself is a separate hiring/promotion pipeline question.

1a. Reveal Simpson’s paradox with tidy verbs

Compute overall mean salary by group, then stratify by dept and group using group_by() and summarize().

Show solution 
salaries |> group_by(group) |> summarize(n = n(), mean_sal = mean(salary_k))

salaries |>
  group_by(dept, group) |>
  summarize(n = n(), mean_sal = mean(salary_k))

1b. Adjusted vs. unadjusted linear model

Fit lm(salary_k ~ group) and lm(salary_k ~ group + dept) and compare the groupGroup_B coefficient.

Show solution 
coef(lm(salary_k ~ group, data = salaries))
coef(lm(salary_k ~ group + dept, data = salaries))
Case Study 2: Laptop vs. Longhand Note-Taking (Experiments & Randomization Inference)
Dataset: notes_study (30 randomized students)

30 students are randomly assigned to take notes by method = "Longhand" ($n=15$) or "Laptop" ($n=15$) during a recorded lecture, then take a conceptual quiz (quiz_score). Pre-experiment GPA (prior_gpa) was measured before assignment.

Balance check: Longhand mean GPA = 3.158 | Laptop mean GPA = 3.353
Observed ATE (Longhand - Laptop): +5.45 quiz points
Question 2.1

Define potential outcomes $(Y_i(\text{Longhand}), Y_i(\text{Laptop}))$ for student $i$. Why can we estimate the Average Treatment Effect ($\text{ATE}$) even though we never observe both outcomes for any student?

Show answer
Each student has two potential quiz scores, one if assigned Longhand and one if assigned Laptop, only one of which is realized. Under random assignment, the treated and control groups are comparable in expectation across all observed and unobserved traits (verified on prior_gpa), so $\bar{Y}_{\text{Longhand}} - \bar{Y}_{\text{Laptop}}$ is an unbiased estimator of $\text{ATE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (Y_i(\text{Longhand}) - Y_i(\text{Laptop}))$.
Question 2.2

State the sharp null hypothesis and explain why it lets us generate the exact permutation distribution without knowing the students’ true missing potential outcomes.

Show answer
The sharp null states that $H_0: Y_i(\text{Longhand}) = Y_i(\text{Laptop})$ for every student $i$. Under $H_0$, changing a student’s assignment leaves their quiz score unchanged, so simply shuffling method labels across the fixed vector of scores yields another equally likely randomization draw under the null.

2a. Check covariate balance and compute observed ATE

Summarize prior_gpa and quiz_score by method, then compute obs_ate.

Show solution 
notes_study |>
  group_by(method) |>
  summarize(n = n(), mean_gpa = mean(prior_gpa), mean_score = mean(quiz_score))

obs_ate <- with(notes_study, mean(quiz_score[method == "Longhand"]) - mean(quiz_score[method == "Laptop"]))
obs_ate

2b. Run a 1,000-replicate permutation test

Set set.seed(20), permute method 1,000 times, and compute the two-sided $p$-value.

Show solution 
set.seed(20)
perm_ates <- replicate(1000, {
  shuffled <- sample(notes_study$method)
  mean(notes_study$quiz_score[shuffled == "Longhand"]) - mean(notes_study$quiz_score[shuffled == "Laptop"])
})
mean(abs(perm_ates) >= abs(obs_ate))
Case Study 3: Campus Gym Policy Change (Difference-in-Differences)
Dataset: gym_warmup (80 observations across North/South Campus, Pre/Post)

North_Campus (treated = 1) opens an hour earlier starting in the post period (post = 1), while South_Campus (treated = 0) keeps existing hours. Mid-semester exam workload causes overall attendance to dip in the post period.

DID = (4.354 - 4.560) - (2.389 - 3.173) = +0.577 visits/wk
Question 3.1

North Campus visits fell slightly from Pre to Post (4.56 to 4.35). Why does a naive before-and-after comparison give the wrong sign?

Show answer
Before-and-after ignores the campus-wide seasonal dip (-0.78 visits/wk at South Campus). Relative to South Campus’s downward trend, North Campus attendance stayed higher by +0.58 visits/wk.
Question 3.2

What is the parallel trends assumption here, and describe one concrete violation.

Show answer
Parallel trends assumes that in the absence of the extended-hours policy, North Campus would have experienced the same average Pre-to-Post change in visits as South Campus. It would be violated if North Campus opened a new dorm or canceled a competing class schedule right when post = 1 began.

3a. Compute the 2×2 cell means and DID manually

Use group_by(treated, post) |> summarize(...) and subtract the control change from the treated change.

Show solution 
cells <- gym_warmup |>
  group_by(treated, post) |>
  summarize(n = n(), mean_visits = mean(visits_wk))
cells
did_est <- (cells$mean_visits[4] - cells$mean_visits[3]) - (cells$mean_visits[2] - cells$mean_visits[1])
did_est
