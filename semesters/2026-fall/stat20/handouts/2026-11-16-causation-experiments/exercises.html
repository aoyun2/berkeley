Piece-by-Piece Breakdown: R Pipelines for Causal Inference & Permutation Tests

Every experiment workflow combines three tools: group_by() + summarize() for group means and covariate balance, lm() for confounding adjustment, and infer (or shuffled vectors) for randomization tests.

# 1. Group summary + observed difference in means (ATE / balance check)
obs_diff <- study_df |>
  group_by(group) |>
  summarize(n = n(), mean_score = mean(score)) |>
  summarize(ate_hat = diff(mean_score))

# 2. Permutation null distribution & two-sided p-value (infer workflow)
obs_stat  <- study_df |> specify(score ~ group) |> calculate(stat = "diff in means", order = c("pdf", "website"))
null_dist <- study_df |> specify(score ~ group) |> hypothesize(null = "independence") |>
  generate(reps = 500, type = "permute") |> calculate(stat = "diff in means", order = c("pdf", "website"))
null_dist |> get_p_value(obs_stat = obs_stat, direction = "both")

Case Study 1: Tech Company Salary Audit (Confounding & Simpson’s Paradox)

Dataset: salaries (40 employees across Engineering & Operations)

An audit checks salary (salary_k, in $1000s) for Group_A vs. Group_B across two departments (dept). Comparing pooled averages directly can mislead when group sizes across high-paying and lower-paying departments differ sharply.

Operations:  Group_A mean = 87.67k (n=4), Group_B mean = 93.79k (n=14)   -> Group_B +6.12k
lm(salary_k ~ group + dept) coefficient on groupGroup_B: +4.30k
Question 1.1

Group_B earns a higher mean salary inside Engineering and inside Operations, yet a lower mean salary overall. How is that possible?

Show answer
This is Simpson’s paradox driven by confounding by dept. Engineering pays ~$149k vs. ~$93k in Operations, and 18/22 of Group_A works in Engineering vs. only 4/18 of Group_B. The pooled average mixes department composition with within-department pay.
Question 1.2

Explain the causal role of dept when auditing pay disparities: why does conditioning on dept answer a different question than the unadjusted difference?

Show answer
Conditioning on dept gives a within-department conditional association (+4.30k for Group_B holding dept fixed). Treating that regression as causal proof requires an explicit causal model: if department choice precedes group or reflects separate sorting, dept acts as a confounder, whereas if group status affects department placement/promotion, dept is a mediator on the causal pathway for total disparity. Moreover, unobserved determinants such as job level and tenure are absent here.

1a. Reveal Simpson’s paradox with tidy verbs

Compute overall mean salary by group, then stratify by dept and group using group_by() and summarize().

Show solution 
sal_by_group <- salaries |> group_by(group) |> summarize(n = n(), mean_sal = mean(salary_k))
sal_by_group

sal_by_dept <- salaries |>
  group_by(dept, group) |>
  summarize(n = n(), mean_sal = mean(salary_k))
sal_by_dept

1b. Adjusted vs. unadjusted linear model

Fit lm(salary_k ~ group) and lm(salary_k ~ group + dept) and compare the groupGroup_B coefficient.

Show solution

m_sal_adj <- lm(salary_k ~ group + dept, data = salaries)

Case Study 2: Laptop vs. Longhand Note-Taking (Experiments & Randomization Inference)

Dataset: notes_study (30 randomized students)

30 students are randomly assigned to take notes by method = "Longhand" ($n=15$) or "Laptop" ($n=15$) during a recorded lecture, then take a conceptual quiz (quiz_score). Pre-experiment GPA (prior_gpa) was measured before assignment.

Observed ATE (Longhand - Laptop): +5.45 quiz points
Question 2.1

Define potential outcomes $(Y_i(\text{Longhand}), Y_i(\text{Laptop}))$ for student $i$. Why can we estimate the Average Treatment Effect ($\text{ATE}$) even though we never observe both outcomes for any student?

Show answer
Each student has two potential quiz scores, one if assigned Longhand and one if assigned Laptop, only one of which is realized. Random assignment guarantees comparability in expectation across both observed and unobserved baseline traits by design, so $\bar{Y}_{\text{Longhand}} - \bar{Y}_{\text{Laptop}}$ is an unbiased estimator of $\text{ATE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (Y_i(\text{Longhand}) - Y_i(\text{Laptop}))$. Comparing prior_gpa (3.158 vs. 3.353) is only a realized balance check on one observed variable and cannot verify balance on unobserved traits.
Question 2.2

State the sharp null hypothesis and explain why it lets us generate the exact permutation distribution without knowing the students’ true missing potential outcomes.

Show answer
The sharp null states that $H_0: Y_i(\text{Longhand}) = Y_i(\text{Laptop})$ for every student $i$. Under $H_0$, changing a student’s assignment leaves their quiz score unchanged, so simply shuffling method labels across the fixed vector of scores yields another equally likely randomization draw under the null.

2a. Check covariate balance and compute observed ATE

Summarize prior_gpa and quiz_score by method, then compute obs_ate.

Show solution 
notes_bal <- notes_study |>
  group_by(method) |>
  summarize(n = n(), mean_gpa = mean(prior_gpa), mean_score = mean(quiz_score))
notes_bal

obs_ate <- with(notes_study, mean(quiz_score[method == "Longhand"]) - mean(quiz_score[method == "Laptop"]))
obs_ate

2b. Run a 1,000-replicate permutation test (Monte Carlo vs. exact null)

Set set.seed(20) and permute method 1,000 times. Compute both the raw Monte Carlo proportion p_mc = exceed / 1000 and the add-one estimate p_add1 = (exceed + 1) / (1000 + 1); note that seeing 0 exceedances reflects finite $B = 1000$ sampling resolution (~0.001), whereas exact dynamic-programming enumeration across all $\binom{30}{15} = 155{,}117{,}520$ splits yields $79{,}434$ extreme assignments ($p_{\text{exact}} \approx 0.000512$).

Show solution 
set.seed(20)
perm_ates <- replicate(1000, {
  shuffled <- sample(notes_study$method)
  mean(notes_study$quiz_score[shuffled == "Longhand"]) - mean(notes_study$quiz_score[shuffled == "Laptop"])
})
exceed <- sum(abs(perm_ates) >= abs(obs_ate))
p_mc <- exceed / 1000
p_add1 <- (exceed + 1) / (1000 + 1)
c(exceed = exceed, p_mc = p_mc, p_add1 = p_add1)

North_Campus (treated = 1) opens an hour earlier starting in the post period (post = 1), while South_Campus (treated = 0) keeps existing hours. Mid-semester exam workload causes overall attendance to dip in the post period.

Question 3.1

North Campus visits fell slightly from Pre to Post (4.56 to 4.35). Why does a naive before-and-after comparison give the wrong sign?

Show answer
Before-and-after ignores the campus-wide seasonal dip (-0.78 visits/wk at South Campus). Relative to South Campus’s downward trend, North Campus attendance stayed higher by +0.58 visits/wk.
Question 3.2

What is the parallel trends assumption here, and describe one concrete violation.

Show answer
Parallel trends assumes that in the absence of the extended-hours policy, North Campus would have experienced the same average Pre-to-Post change in visits as South Campus. It would be violated if North Campus opened a new dorm or canceled a competing class schedule right when post = 1 began.

3a. Compute the 2×2 cell means and DID manually

Use group_by(treated, post) |> summarize(...) and subtract the control change from the treated change.

Show solution 
cells <- gym_warmup |>
  group_by(treated, post) |>
  summarize(n = n(), mean_visits = mean(visits_wk))
cells
did_est <- (cells$mean_visits[4] - cells$mean_visits[3]) - (cells$mean_visits[2] - cells$mean_visits[1])

Case Study 4: Independent Transfer — Clinic Outreach Dataset

Simulated Dataset: outreach_sim (40 clinic-month observations across Clinic_A and Clinic_B, pre and post)

Clinic_A (treated = 1) begins extended weekend outreach starting at post = 1, while Clinic_B (treated = 0) keeps baseline hours. Both clinics also experience a seasonal winter drop in checkups from pre to post, and started at different baseline volumes (Clinic_B pre = 40.65, post = 34.81; Clinic_A pre = 29.80, post = 26.95).

4. Choose an estimator and interpret the result

Choose an appropriate estimator for the effect of extended hours on weekly checkups and assign your scalar result to outreach_est. Explain in your own words why comparing post-period clinics directly or taking only Clinic_A's before-and-after change gives the wrong sign.

Show solution & self-review criteria 
outreach_cells <- outreach_sim |>
  group_by(treated, post) |>
  summarize(n = n(), mean_chk = mean(checkups))
outreach_est <- (outreach_cells$mean_chk[4] - outreach_cells$mean_chk[3]) -
  (outreach_cells$mean_chk[2] - outreach_cells$mean_chk[1])
outreach_est
Self-review interpretation: Comparing post means alone yields -7.86 (confounded by Clinic_A's lower baseline), and before-and-after at Clinic_A alone yields -2.85 (confounded by the seasonal dip). Subtracting Clinic_B's change (-5.84) estimates +2.99 checkups, assuming parallel trends in the absence of outreach.
