Piece-by-Piece Breakdown: Prediction, Bootstrap, and Classification in R

Weeks 9–12 rely on three recurring R templates: fitting and evaluating OLS models (lm + predict), drawing size-$n$ resamples with replacement (sample(..., replace = TRUE) / infer), and scoring binary classifiers (glm(..., family = "binomial") + type = "response").

# A. Train OLS & score held-out RMSE / R^2
m_fit <- lm(y ~ poly(x, 2, raw = TRUE), data = train_df)
test_scored <- test_df |> mutate(y_hat = predict(m_fit, newdata = test_df), err = y - y_hat)

# B. Bootstrap percentile interval in base R (size n, WITH replacement)
boot_stats <- replicate(500, mean(sample(x_vec, size = length(x_vec), replace = TRUE)))
quantile(boot_stats, probs = c(0.025, 0.975))

# C. Fit logistic regression & convert predicted probabilities to 0/1 classes
m_cls <- glm(y_01 ~ x1 + x2, data = train_df, family = "binomial")
cls_scored <- test_df |> mutate(p_hat = predict(m_cls, newdata = test_df, type = "response"),
                                y_hat = ifelse(p_hat > 0.5, 1, 0))

Question 1.1

Interpret the slope and intercept in context. Should we trust the model to predict cone sales on a winter day with temp_f = 30$^\circ\text{F}$?

Show answer
Each $1^\circ\text{F}$ increase in daily high temperature is associated with an average increase of 5.08 cones sold. The intercept (-221.04) predicts sales at $0^\circ\text{F}$ and has no physical meaning. Predicting at $30^\circ\text{F}$ is unsafe extrapolation well outside the observed 66--91$^\circ\text{F}$ training range (and gives negative sales).
Question 1.2

Compute the predicted value and residual $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$ for day 3 in icecream (temp_f = 72, cones = 145).

Show answer
Using $\hat{y} = -221.04 + 5.08(72) \approx 144.97$, the residual is $e_3 = 145 - 144.97 = +0.03$. Because it is positive, the vendor sold slightly more cones than the regression line predicted.
Question 1.3

Why evaluate prediction error (RMSE) on new_icecream instead of on icecream?

Show answer
Training residuals are minimized by OLS on the same observations used to fit the parameters, which understates out-of-sample error. Evaluating on held-out days in new_icecream measures how accurately the fitted line predicts unseen days (expected test RMSE $\approx 1.88$ cones).

Question 2.1

Why does adding I(speed_mph^2) reduce test RMSE so sharply relative to m_lin?

Show answer
Total stopping distance combines reaction distance (linear in speed) and braking distance (quadratic in speed, proportional to kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$). A straight line underpredicts at both low and high speeds while overpredicting in the middle; adding I(speed_mph^2) removes this systematic bias.
Question 2.2

In m_quad, how do you interpret the coefficient on wet_road ($\approx 28.2$ ft)?

Show answer
Holding speed_mph fixed, a wet road adds about 28.2 ft to predicted stopping distance compared with a dry road.

Question 3.1

What does the age_yr:hybrid interaction term measure?

Show answer
It measures how the yearly depreciation slope differs between hybrids and gas cars. Since age_yr has slope -1.84 for gas cars and age_yr:hybrid is +0.78, hybrids lose $0.78k less value per year of age (i.e., depreciate more slowly).
Question 3.2

A student fits degree-6 polynomials in age interacted with hybrid plus degree-3 polynomials in mileage. Training $R^2$ reaches $0.996$ (training RMSE $0.415$), yet held-out test RMSE ($0.953$) widens compared to the training fit and slightly trails the interaction model ($0.913$). Diagnose why.

Show answer
On $n = 24$ training cars, a rank-17 design matrix has almost as many parameters as observations, driving training RMSE down to $0.415$ while test RMSE on these 12 cars is $0.953$ (vs. $0.913$ for the much simpler 5-parameter interaction model). That train–test gap reflects overfitting to training noise, even though on this small split test error only modestly exceeds the interaction model rather than exploding.

Question 4.1

In bootstrap resampling, why do we sample $n$ observations with replacement, and what changes if we increase $B$ from $500$ to $2000$ resamples?

Show answer
Sampling $n$ items with replacement mimics drawing fresh samples of size $n$ from the population using the empirical sample as our plug-in population. Increasing $B$ reduces Monte Carlo simulation jitter in the histogram/quantiles, but it does not shrink the standard error (which depends on sample size $n$, roughly $s/\sqrt{n}$) and cannot remove sampling bias.
Question 4.2

Suppose a 95% bootstrap percentile CI for the Adelie mean bill length is $[L, U]$. Is it true that 95% of all Adelie penguins have bill lengths between $L$ and $U$?

Show answer
No. The interval targets the population mean bill length of Adelie penguins, not individual penguins. The individual distribution is much wider (around $\pm 2 s \approx \pm 5.0$ mm).

Question 5.1

Interpret the exp_years coefficient on both the log-odds scale and the odds ratio scale. Why is $\hat{\beta}_1$ not a constant change in probability?

Show answer
Holding honors fixed, one extra year of experience increases the log-odds of a callback by 0.390, which multiplies the odds by $e^{0.390} \approx 1.48$. Because the logistic curve $\sigma(\eta) = 1/(1+e^{-\eta})$ is S-shaped, a fixed step in log-odds changes probability most near $p = 0.5$ and very little near $0$ or $1$.
Question 5.2

Explain the tradeoff between precision and recall if we lower the classification threshold from $0.5$ to $0.3$.

Show answer
Lowering the threshold predicts positive (y_hat = 1) more often, reducing false negatives (so recall increases or stays the same) while typically increasing false positives (which tends to lower precision).
